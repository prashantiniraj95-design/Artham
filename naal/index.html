Before the collection

The paai. A familiar part of every home. Spread out for sleeping, rolled out for festivals and gatherings, then tucked away behind the door until the next time.
  1. Unrolled on the cool floor after lunch, the whole house asleep by two.
  2. You woke with its weave printed on your cheek.

Artham

meaning

Artham began on paper, with patterns drawn from whatever was already in view. Tile. Shadow. Grain. The grid of a window at four in the afternoon.

Bags came later, and only because the paai was never exotic to us. One of us reads pattern for a living. The other grew up a short road from the looms, where a mat is simply a mat.

Between those two habits of seeing, the weave was translated. It was carried across into a different object, with its ordinariness intact.

In Pathamadai, a village on the banks of the Thamirabarani in Tirunelveli, korai grass has been woven into paai for generations, fine enough, it is said, to fold like silk.

The grass is split by hand, soaked, dyed and woven into the mats that are slept on, sat on, unrolled for guests, folded under an arm on the way to the market. At its finest the weave is worked wet, thread by thread, until a mat folds like cloth.

It is a household craft there, not a rarity. Artham works alongside those weavers to carry it into the everyday. We have changed only what it is asked to hold.

Craftsmanship

From the riverbank to your hand

  1. 01

    Grown

    Korai, a slender sedge, grows thick along the banks of the Thamirabarani.

  2. 02

    Split by hand

    Each stem is split into fine strands. The finer the split, the finer the mat.

  3. 03

    Soaked & dyed

    Softened in water, dyed in batches: wine, black, and the jewelled colours kept back for festivals.

  4. 04

    Woven wet

    On the loom, worked wet, thread by thread, the weft beaten close until the mat folds like cloth.

  5. 05

    Cut & stitched

    The finished paai is cut, framed in leather and stitched with a hand-finished edge.

  6. 06

    Carried

    Out of the house, onto the shoulder, into the day. Made in limited number.

Veyil

வெயில் · sunlight

Every household keeps a katta pai by the door, the one grabbed without thinking on the way out for a market run in the afternoon sun.

Folded under an arm, bright enough to be picked out from the end of the street. Veyil keeps that brightness, and that way of being carried: a circle cut clean through leather, the weave burning through the opening.

Chapter II  ·  Afternoon  ·  15:30

II

Nizhal

நிழல் · shade

By afternoon the light has moved. It comes through the grill and lies across the tiled floor in squares, and the house goes quiet behind it.

Too warm to be outside, quiet enough that the tiles hold the light in patches. Nizhal is that pause. It takes the chequer onto the shoulder: long, low, and unhurried, the way the hour is.

Chapter III  ·  Evening  ·  18:30

III

Malai

மாலை · evening

A paai, laid out flat, holds an entire gathering: food and conversation, cards and coffee, people settling in around it.

Malai is the mat unrolled when people come: black and beige, the paai in its oldest colouring, the one nobody thinks to call a pattern. It holds what a day asks you to carry, and it does not wait for evening to do it.

Chapter IV  ·  Festival night  ·  21:00

Vizha

விழா · celebration

Some colours are kept back. They are not for the floor or the afternoon; they come out of the cupboard for weddings, for temple nights, for the days a house fills up.

Some nights aren't ordinary, and the bag shouldn't be either. Vizha is woven in those colours, jewelled greens, magentas and wine, in two pieces made to be held rather than worn.

Made by hand

No two quite alike.

The weave carries the hand that made it: a slightly different tension, a dye batch a shade warmer. Those aren't flaws. They're how you know a person was there.

  • Korai

    Grass from the banks of the Thamirabarani, split, soaked and dyed by hand.

  • Woven

    On the loom in Pathamadai, by weavers for whom this has always been household work.

  • Leather

    Cut, edged and stitched by hand around each weave, finished with the Artham mark.

  • Limited

    Made in limited number. When a weave runs out, the next bag is a little different.

Caring for it, like a paai

  • Keep it dry; if it meets the rain, pat it down and let it dry in the shade, never the sun.
  • Brush the weave with a soft dry cloth. Korai softens and glows with use.
  • Store it filled with paper, in its cloth bag. Rolled out for the day, tucked away until the next time.

The founders

Prashantini & Dhiraj

Artham is two people and one habit of seeing.

We started on paper, drawing patterns from whatever was already in view: the tile underfoot, the shadow of a grill, the grain of a door. One of us reads pattern for a living. The other grew up a short road from the looms, where a mat is simply a mat.

Naal is our first collection, and the most personal thing we know how to make: the paai from every house we grew up in, carried out of the door and into the day. We work alongside the weavers of Pathamadai, and we make each bag in limited number, slowly and by hand.
